# Textbook Examples Are Core Teaching Material — Not Decorations

This example is a supplement to `SKILL.md`; it is a teaching procedure, **not a quotation from the textbook**.

## Start with the user's actual stumbling block

If the book lists many expressions with *dog* and the student cannot interpret them, do not write “dogs have cultural connotations” and move on. Explain every expression before generalizing.

| Original English expression | Literal image | Conventional meaning | Why it is in the lesson |
|---|---|---|---|
| Every dog has his day. | Every dog gets one day. | Everyone gets their chance or period of success. | Animal word used figuratively to describe human fortune. |
| Let sleeping dogs lie. | Do not disturb sleeping dogs. | Don't revive an old controversy or problem. | A concrete scenario motivates an abstract admonition. |
| Love me, love my dog. | If you love me, love my dog too. | Accept things and people associated with someone you love. | The literal animal can be understood figuratively. |
| A lucky dog | A fortunate dog | A lucky person | The noun *dog* may refer figuratively to a human. |
| Lead a dog's life | Live a dog's life | Live miserably, under hardship | Demonstrates a negative usage, so never conclude that *dog* is uniformly positive in English. |

For a long example list, follow the user's page in order and do not silently omit difficult items. Cite page and position. When unsure of an idiom's meaning, verify it rather than invent a gloss.

## Conceptual metaphor: STEAM, FIRE, and ANGER

Explicitly state that this is an **outside scholarly interpretation** (cognitive linguistics), not necessarily the textbook author's developed explanation.

1. **Textbook phenomenon**: speakers use heat, fire, gas, or pressure expressions to talk about anger. Chinese: 怒火中烧 (anger as a burning fire), 一肚子气 (anger conceptualized via qi/gas); English: *His anger was smoldering*, *She was boiling with anger*, *He let off steam*.
2. **First explain the literal scene**: a fire smolders, a liquid reaches boiling point, pressure is released as steam. None of these requires a literal fire inside the person.
3. **Next explain the figurative sense**: smoldering = restrained but persistent anger; boiling = intensely angry; let off steam = release pent-up emotion.
4. **Only then introduce theory**: A conceptual metaphor involves a systematic mapping between a relatively concrete **source domain** (FIRE, HOT LIQUID or GAS UNDER PRESSURE) and an abstract **target domain** (ANGER). Increasing heat/pressure can correspond to increasing anger intensity; release corresponds to emotional release.
5. **Different mappings may coexist**: *ANGER IS FIRE* and *ANGER IS HOT FLUID IN A CONTAINER* are distinguishable but can share the broader *ANGER IS HEAT* pattern. Do not pretend the book authored a formal hierarchy. The generalization is an analytic reconstruction drawn from cognitive linguistics.
6. **Evidence limit**: The existence of linguistic metaphors does not, by itself, prove that a culture is emotionally or neurologically different. Corpus patterns, historical sources or controlled experiments may be necessary for stronger claims.

Research sources for the external extension:
- Lakoff & Kövecses (1987), “The cognitive model of anger inherent in American English,” in *Cultural Models in Language and Thought* (Cambridge University Press), https://doi.org/10.1017/CBO9780511607660.009
- Ning Yu (1995), “Metaphorical Expressions of Anger and Happiness in English and Chinese,” *Metaphor and Symbolic Activity*, 10(2), 59–92, https://doi.org/10.1207/s15327868ms1002_1

## Mandatory close for a passage

After interpreting each example, state what is **directly observed**, what **the author claims**, what **the external theory can explain**, and what **remains unproven**. This progression is more useful than repeated one-sentence section summaries.
